The work

The Amsterdam menus study

In July 2026 I published a study of Amsterdam's restaurant menus: a June 2026 snapshot of 32,405 dishes across 930 venues, about 4 in 10 of the city's restaurants. It is built only from public data and a model whose one job is to refuse unfair comparisons: never a steakhouse against a snackbar, never a dessert against a main. Here are six of its findings, as the study states them.

Snapshot · June 2026

An ordinary dinner: €35–38, everywhere

A standard evening out, a burrata to start, a pasta main, a cheesecake after, lands at €35 to €38 in every part of the city. No neighbourhood breaks away by more than the noise. What you pay is decided by what you order, far more than by where you sit.

Snapshot · June 2026

Prices follow where tourists stay

Dishes cost about 4.2% more per step up in tourist exposure (the honest range: +1.8 to +6.6). The premium follows where tourists sleep, not distance to landmarks. The study shows where it sits, not why.

Snapshot · June 2026

The dish sets the price, not the venue

59.5% of price variation is which dish it is; only 16.8% is which venue serves it. Most of what looks like venue pricing power is dish mix.

Snapshot · June 2026

Cafés run 11.2% below the market

The same dish in a café setting prices 11.2% lower (the honest range: −14.7 to −7.6). Where you sit changes what you pay.

Snapshot · June 2026

Steakhouses crowd the centre

They appear in the city centre 2.6 times as often as in the rest of the city. Knowing where a concept crowds also tells you where it is absent.

Snapshot · June 2026

One bitterbal, a fourfold spread

The same snack at 138 venues: median €1.33 a piece, from €0.68 to €2.67. Hold the product constant and what is left is pure location.

From one study

The questions it answers

The study covers one market and one set of questions, but the method travels: any market where menus, prices and venues can be observed, and any claim that has to survive a skeptical reader. These are examples of what work like this hands you, depending on where you sit. It is work for companies on hospitality data, not for individual restaurants or restaurant groups.

For field-sales platforms and distributors

What can your reps say in front of a venue?

A price reference your people can quote: what is typical for a dish, what counts as expensive, where a venue sits against its true peers. A rep who knows the market better than the venue does starts a different conversation.

For companies that hold data

What could your data become?

POS systems, payment platforms and data providers sit on data their clients would pay to understand. I tell you which insight products yours could carry, which claims would survive a skeptical reader, and what it takes to turn a raw corpus into a dataset your team can build on.

For wholesalers and brands

Where is the white space?

Which concepts crowd which neighbourhoods, and where a category is absent, in plain counts, so you know where to point a salesforce or launch a product.

For anyone about to act on a number

Will this finding survive scrutiny?

Before you publish a number or build on one, I stress-test it the way my own published work is tested: the threat, the test, the verdict, one line each. If it breaks, better it breaks with me.

These are examples, not boundaries. If your question looks like none of them, ask anyway.

The spin-off

The dish price atlas

The study also left behind a tool: a browsable atlas of Amsterdam dish prices. Pick a dish, and you get the typical price and the range where 9 out of 10 land; rare dishes get a sensible range too, not a made-up one. It is the same work from a different angle: not an argument to read, a reference to use. The reference tables I build in an engagement take exactly this shape.

How work starts

A rough question is enough

I do not pitch cold and I do not run outbound, so the first step is yours. It is a smaller step than it looks: there is no sales team behind this page, only the person who does the work, happy to think along before anything is agreed.

  1. Write me. Email or LinkedIn, whichever is easier. You do not need a polished brief: two sentences about what you are trying to decide, or about what your data will not tell you, is plenty. Within a day I tell you whether I am the right person for it. If I am not, I say so.
  2. We talk. The conversation costs nothing and commits you to nothing. Bring doubts rather than specifications; sorting them out together is what the call is for. I do not quote numbers on calls.
  3. You get a written proposal: what can be done and how, what cannot be done and why, a fixed scope, a fixed duration, and one fixed price.
  4. You decide with the document in hand, and share it with whoever needs to see it.

If you are a fund, an agency or a platform looking at this for a portfolio company or a client, the same email works.

The standing terms, the same for everyone:

  • One engagement at a time. Start dates are agreed when the fee is agreed.
  • Fixed fee. One number, in writing, before anything starts. No hourly billing, no overage, no surprises. If the work takes longer than I estimated, that is my risk.
  • Payment. 50% on start, 50% on delivery. Invoiced from the Netherlands; VAT handled according to where your business is registered.
  • Confidentiality. Your data stays yours and is used only for your engagement. It is never resold, never passed on, and never used as a case study without your written permission.
